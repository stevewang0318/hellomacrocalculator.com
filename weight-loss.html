How the Mifflin–St Jeor estimate works ＋

1. Estimate resting energy (RMR). The calculator uses your age, height, weight, and selected sex value in the Mifflin–St Jeor equation[1]:

  • Female equation: RMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161
  • Male equation: RMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5

RMR is an estimate of energy used at rest; it is not your total daily calorie target.

2. Estimate daily expenditure (TDEE). We multiply estimated RMR by the activity factor you select: sedentary 1.2; light 1.375; moderate 1.55; high 1.725; very high 1.9. These are practical estimation factors, not direct measurements of your activity.

3. Apply your goal. The calculator adjusts estimated TDEE for your selected goal and pace, then distributes calories across protein, fat, and carbohydrates based on your chosen preferences. For more clinical background, see Medscape’s Mifflin–St Jeor calculator explanation[6].

Predictive equations can differ from measured energy expenditure. Use the result as a starting estimate, not an exact measurement or medical prescription.

HOW TO CALCULATE MACROS FOR WEIGHT LOSS

Understand the calorie deficit.

Start with energy needs

Weight loss requires an energy deficit over time, but the size of that deficit should be workable. This calculator estimates resting energy with the Mifflin–St Jeor equation[1] and scales it by your reported activity to estimate total daily energy expenditure (TDEE). Your selected rate then sets a moderate target deficit, capped to avoid translating an aggressive pace into an extreme calorie cut.

The result is a planning estimate rather than a promise. Energy expenditure equations cannot account for every difference in movement, digestion, sleep, health, or tracking accuracy. A measured weight trend over several weeks is more informative than a single predicted number.

Protein helps make the plan practical

Protein is set from your chosen grams-per-kilogram preference. A higher preference can be useful for people who lift weights or want a more filling meal pattern, but more is not automatically better for everyone. Divide the daily amount across meals in a way that suits your routine. If you have kidney disease, are pregnant, or follow a medically prescribed diet, ask a qualified professional before changing intake.

Fat and carbohydrates have room to flex

Your fat preference establishes a daily amount first. Carbohydrates then receive the remaining calorie budget, so the split responds to your energy target and protein/fat choices instead of assuming one universal “fat loss ratio.” Carbs can support training and everyday food preferences; fat contributes energy and helps make meals satisfying.

You can set a custom ratio if you have a reason to follow one. Adult reference ranges provide context, not personalized prescriptions[3]. The calculator translates percentages into grams using 4 kcal per gram for protein and carbohydrates and 9 kcal per gram for fat. Treat those numbers as a useful structure, not a scorecard. Food quality, fiber, micronutrients, sleep, and sustainable habits matter too.

What to do with your result

Use consistent weigh-ins and compare weekly averages for 2–3 weeks, where appropriate. If the trend is consistently faster or slower than intended, consider a small 100–200 kcal/day adjustment and reassess. Day-to-day scale changes often reflect water, digestion, and other normal variation. You do not need to hit every gram perfectly to learn from the trend.

Resistance training, adequate recovery, and a pace you can maintain can help make a fat-loss plan more supportive of strength and lean mass. This tool does not diagnose, treat, or replace individual medical or dietetic advice.

How to use this calculator

Choose the activity level that best reflects your usual week, then set a weekly pace you can realistically maintain. Protein and fat preferences shape those targets; carbohydrates receive the remaining calories. Optional body-fat input switches to the fat-free-mass equation commonly called Katch–McArdle[5], so use it only if you have a reasonably recent estimate.

How to read your results

The calorie target is an estimate for your selected rate of loss. BMR estimates energy at rest; TDEE estimates daily expenditure after activity is considered. Macro grams show a daily distribution, while percentages show each macro’s share of calories. Neither is a score. Rounded macro grams may not add up exactly to the displayed calorie target.

A practical example

Imagine a sample target of 120 g protein, 200 g carbs, and 65 g fat. You might spread protein across four meals at roughly 30 g each, while allowing carbohydrate and fat amounts to vary with your schedule and appetite. This only illustrates how to distribute a daily total; it is not a recommended target or meal plan for every person.

How to adjust over time

Compare consistent weigh-ins as weekly averages over 2–3 weeks. Daily changes can reflect water, digestion, sodium, menstrual cycles, and training. If the longer trend is consistently faster or slower than intended, consider a small 100–200 kcal/day adjustment and reassess. If tracking feels distressing, stop and seek professional support.

For adults 18+. Estimates only, not medical advice or a precise measure of physiological requirements. Seek individualized guidance from a registered dietitian or clinician.
WEIGHT LOSS FAQ

Good questions.
Clear answers.

How to calculate macros for weight loss?＋

Estimate daily energy expenditure, choose a sustainable calorie deficit, set protein and fat targets based on body weight and preferences, then allocate the remaining calories to carbohydrates. Reassess with your weight trend over several weeks.

What is a good macro ratio for weight loss?＋

There is no single best ratio for everyone[4]. A useful plan accounts for total calories, adequate protein, a personally workable fat intake, food preferences, and training. This calculator derives the remaining carbohydrate target from those choices.

How many macros should I eat to lose weight?＋

Your targets depend on body size, estimated activity, pace, and preferences. Enter those details above to get an estimate. Treat it as a starting range to refine, not an exact physiological requirement.

Do I need to count macros to lose weight?＋

No. Some people prefer tracking, while others use portions, meal routines, or clinician-supported approaches. Macro tracking is an optional way to make intake more visible; choose a method that supports your well-being.

What should my macros be for cutting?＋

“Cutting” usually means eating below estimated energy needs while aiming to maintain training and adequate nutrition. The same principles apply: a measured deficit, a sensible protein target, and enough flexibility to follow the plan consistently.

How quickly should I expect to lose weight?＋

Progress varies and is not perfectly linear. The tool caps the estimated deficit, but the scale can fluctuate. Look at several weeks of trend data and seek professional advice if you have a medical condition or a history of disordered eating.
