How the Mifflin–St Jeor estimate works ＋

1. Estimate resting energy (RMR). The calculator uses your age, height, weight, and selected sex value in the Mifflin–St Jeor equation[1]:

  • Female equation: RMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161
  • Male equation: RMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5

RMR is an estimate of energy used at rest; it is not your total daily calorie target.

2. Estimate daily expenditure (TDEE). We multiply estimated RMR by the activity factor you select: sedentary 1.2; light 1.375; moderate 1.55; high 1.725; very high 1.9. These are practical estimation factors, not direct measurements of your activity.

3. Apply your goal. The calculator adjusts estimated TDEE for your selected goal and pace, then distributes calories across protein, fat, and carbohydrates based on your chosen preferences. For more clinical background, see Medscape’s Mifflin–St Jeor calculator explanation[6].

Predictive equations can differ from measured energy expenditure. Use the result as a starting estimate, not an exact measurement or medical prescription.

FROM INPUTS TO EVERYDAY USE

How to use your macro results.

How to use this calculator

Enter your age, height, current weight, and the biological sex value used by the selected BMR equation. Choose the activity level that best reflects your usual week, not your most active day. Then select a goal and a protein and fat preference. Optional body-fat input switches to the fat-free-mass equation commonly called Katch–McArdle[5], so use it only if you have a reasonably recent estimate.

Each choice is an estimate or preference, not a test you can pass or fail. If you are unsure about activity or macro preferences, start with a middle option and use your results as a draft. You can recalculate when your routine or body weight changes.

How to read your results

Your calorie target is the estimated daily energy intake for the goal you selected. BMR estimates energy at rest; TDEE estimates daily expenditure after applying an activity factor. Protein, carbohydrate, and fat grams show how the target calories are distributed. Their percentages describe each macro’s share of macro calories, not a quality rating.

Small differences between the calorie target and calories implied by rounded macro grams are normal. Food labels, fiber, and rounding can also make logged totals differ from the calculator.

A simple way to distribute macros

For example, if a hypothetical target is 120 g protein, 200 g carbohydrates, and 65 g fat, you could spread those amounts across meals according to appetite and schedule. One possible pattern is roughly 30 g protein at four eating occasions, with carbohydrates and fats varying by meal. This is an illustration of distributing a daily total, not a prescribed meal plan or target for everyone.

Foods contain combinations of macros, so each meal does not need to match a perfect ratio. Build meals around foods you enjoy and can access; use tracking only if it is helpful for you.

How to adjust over time

If weight change is part of your goal, compare consistent weigh-ins as weekly averages over about 2–3 weeks. Water, sodium, digestion, menstrual cycles, and training can move the scale day to day. If the longer trend is consistently faster or slower than intended, consider changing the daily calorie target by around 100–200 kcal and reassess after another couple of weeks.

Change one thing at a time and consider energy, hunger, and training performance alongside weight. If tracking or weight monitoring feels distressing, stop and seek support from a qualified professional.

WHAT ARE MACROS?

Three nutrients. One bigger picture.

Macronutrients are the nutrients that provide energy

“Macros” is short for macronutrients: protein, carbohydrates, and fat. Protein and carbohydrates provide about 4 calories per gram; fat provides about 9. Your body uses all three, and each plays a different role in a varied eating pattern[2]. Calories describe energy; macros describe where some of that energy comes from.

Protein supplies amino acids used to build and repair tissues. Carbohydrates are broken down into glucose and can be an important fuel source, especially during higher-intensity activity. Dietary fat supplies energy, supports cell structures, and helps the body absorb fat-soluble vitamins.

How many grams should you eat?

There is no universal ratio that is best for every person or goal[4]. Energy needs, body size, activity, food preferences, health context, and goals all matter. A calculator can estimate a starting calorie target and use protein and fat preferences to distribute those calories; it cannot determine your exact biological needs.

Use this free macro calculator to explore a starting point, then learn how to adapt it on our macro calculator for weight loss page. A consistent, nourishing eating pattern matters more than perfect daily percentages.

GOOD TO KNOW

Frequently asked questions.

What are macros?＋

Macros are protein, carbohydrates, and fat—the macronutrients that provide energy and support different functions in the body. They complement, rather than replace, attention to overall food quality and health.

What is the difference between macros and calories?＋

Calories measure energy. Macros describe the protein, carbohydrate, and fat that contribute that energy. Protein and carbs have about 4 kcal per gram; fat has about 9 kcal per gram.

How many calories are in 1g of protein, carbs and fat?＋

Protein has about 4 kcal per gram, carbohydrates about 4 kcal per gram, and fat about 9 kcal per gram.

How accurate is a macro calculator?＋

It provides estimates from equations and the information you enter. Real energy needs vary. Treat results as a starting point, compare trends over time, and adjust modestly as needed.

Can I calculate macros without an app?＋

Yes. This calculator runs in your browser and shows your estimates without an account or tracking app. You can copy, print, or share the result.

How many grams of protein per day?＋

Protein needs vary with body size, activity, goals, and health context. This tool lets you select a grams-per-kilogram preference to explore a general target; seek individualized guidance where appropriate.

How accurate are these numbers?＋

They are estimates based on equations and the details you provide. Real energy needs vary; use your weight trend, hunger, energy, and performance to refine a starting target.

What does body fat change?＋

Body fat is optional context. This version uses body weight for macro preferences; it does not claim that a body-fat estimate makes calorie targets exact.

How does calorie cycling work?＋

It redistributes the weekly calorie budget across training and rest days. The weekly average remains your daily target.
